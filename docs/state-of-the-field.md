# Dating the surahs: state of knowledge, October 2026

A survey of scholarship on the relative chronology of the Qurʾān, written to accompany this repository (HCSB03H3, University of Toronto Scarborough, 2026) and Prof. Suleyman Dost's chronology project. It is pitched at instructors and advanced students. The plain-language companion to this document is `guide-for-students.md`; the computational results are in `results.md`.

## How to read this report

Every citation below was checked against at least one of: the Crossref DOI registry, the publisher's or repository's own record, the ACL Anthology, or the text of the work itself. Where I read the work itself, I say so. Items I could not fully verify are marked **[UNVERIFIED: …]** inline and collected in the last section. A few page ranges disagree between sources; I give both rather than pick one.

Note on terms used throughout. **Meccan / Medinan**: the traditional Islamic division of surahs (and sometimes single verses) by whether they were proclaimed before or after the Prophet's emigration (hijra) to Medina in 622. **Relative chronology**: an ordering of texts (earlier/later) without calendar dates. **Mean verse length (MVL)**: the average length of a surah's verses, measured by Sinai in letters of a Latin transliteration. **Kufan verse count**: one of several early regional systems for where verse breaks fall; it is the one printed in the Cairo 1924 / Medina muṣḥaf and used by Tanzil, so "verse length" depends on it.

## 1. Suleyman Dost

**Appointment.** The UTSC Department of Historical and Cultural Studies page lists Dost as "Associate Professor of Late Antiquity and Early Islam," Associate Chair (Teaching), and Classical Studies Program Coordinator ([UTSC HCS profile](https://utsc.utoronto.ca/hcs/suleyman-dost)). Other U of T pages (Jackman Humanities Institute, 2025; Department for the Study of Religion) still say "Assistant Professor," so the promotion is recent. He was previously Assistant Professor at Brandeis, and his PhD is from Chicago (2017).

**Dissertation.** *An Arabian Qurʾān: Towards a Theory of Peninsular Origins*. PhD dissertation, University of Chicago, Department of Near Eastern Languages and Civilizations, June 2017. Advisor Fred M. Donner; committee Michael A. Sells and Rebecca Hasselbach-Andee. 309 pp. DOI [10.6082/uchicago.1343](https://doi.org/10.6082/uchicago.1343). It reads the Qurʾān against pre-Islamic Arabian inscriptions (Old South Arabian, Ancient North Arabian, Nabataean, some Palmyrene) and argues against Wansbrough and the "Syriacist" readings, finding lexical and thematic continuity with Peninsular religious culture and particularly with South Arabia and Ethiopia. Chapter 1 compares Qurʾānic divine names with those attested epigraphically. The abstract says nothing about chronology, Meccan/Medinan classification, or verse length.

**"Before the Qur'an" is mainly about epigraphy, and its chapter 3 proposes a chronology.** Suleyman Dost, *Before the Qur'an: Material Sources at the Advent of Muslim Scripture*. Edinburgh Studies in Islamic Scripture and Theology. Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2026. 248 pp. Hardback ISBN 9781399528641; DOI [10.3366/9781399528665](https://doi.org/10.3366/9781399528665) (Crossref gives 31 Jan 2026; EUP's page says February 2026). Per the publisher's description, it is "the first comprehensive reading of the Qur'an through epigraphic sources from the Arabian Peninsula": it uses securely datable pre-Islamic inscriptions to argue that the Qurʾān emerged in the Hijaz and addressed a late antique Arabian audience. It challenges the view that Syriac Christian sources best explain Qurʾānic religious language, emphasises parallels with South Arabian and Ethiopian religious idiom, and explains the Qurʾān's divine names and titles through their epigraphic attestations ([EUP page](https://edinburghuniversitypress.com/book-before-the-qur-an.html)). Chapters: Introduction "Whence the Qur'an?"; 1 Urbanism and Literacy in Pre-Islamic Arabia; 2 Idols of Arabia in Epigraphy and the Qur'an; 3 From Rabb to Allāh: Names of God in the Qur'an; 4 Vestiges of Pagan Arabia in the Qur'an; 5 Religious Vocabulary of the Qur'an; 6 Christian Terms in the Qur'an; 7 History and Historical Geography in the Qur'an; Conclusion "What the Stones can (and cannot) Tell". Chapter 3 sets out a relative chronology; it is summarised in the next paragraph (read in a review copy of the book; page numbers below are the book's printed pages).

**Chapter 3, "From Rabb to Allāh: Names of God in the Qur'an" (pp. 64–92).** Dost accepts Reynolds's objection that chronologies built on the Muslim biographical tradition are circular (p. 65). He argues, though, that treating the Qur'an as "a temporal monolith" ignores readily observable changes, and he adopts Sinai's argument from the covariance of formal and content features (pp. 65–66). He uses Sinai's list of surahs ordered by MVL, with Sinai's fifteen proposed interpolations removed (reproduced as Table 3.1, p. 68), as his chronological guide. He does not use Nöldeke's or the tradition's periods. Reading the divine names along that order, he divides the Qur'an into three "compositional clusters" (pp. 67–80):

- **R-Qur'an** (about 40 surahs, the shortest-verse end of Sinai's list plus Q 54, 68, 69, 70, 73): God is *Rabb-X* ("your Lord", "Lord of …"). Twenty-nine surahs contain no *Allāh* at all. In several more, *Allāh* occurs only in verses Sinai had already marked as insertions (Q 73:20; 74:31, 56; 81:29; 87:7). Dost argues on further grounds that other *Allāh* verses in early surahs are also additions: they break the rhyme (82:19), gloss a *mā adrāka* question (104:6), or add a reminder that God watches the disbelievers (84:23, 85:20, 88:24, 96:14), often following Bell (pp. 69–73). Welch and Robinson had made the same observation earlier (p. 72).
- **AR-Qur'an**: opens with the "Raḥmān surahs" (Q 26, 55, 50, then 36, 20, 43, 19, 67, 21, 25), where *al-Raḥmān* serves as God's proper name and *Allāh* as a generic "god". After Q 25:60 records the audience's rejection of the name, Q 27:30 attributes it to Sheba and Q 17:110 resolves it ("call upon Allāh or al-Raḥmān"). Thereafter *Allāh* and *Rabb-X* appear in roughly equal numbers (pp. 74–77). Markers of this phase include new vocabulary (*nabī*, *ṣirāṭ*, *tawba*; *faṭara* and *ḥizb*, which Jeffery lists as Ethiopic loans), anti-trinitarian polemic ("to take a son"), almost all the surahs with disjointed letters, and every occurrence of *ʿarabī* (pp. 74–78).
- **A-Qur'an** (roughly the traditional Medinan surahs, plus Q 110): *Allāh* is "assuredly" the name of God. Six surahs lack *Rabb-X* entirely, and elsewhere it survives mainly in prayer formulae (*rabbanā*). Almost all A surahs have at least about one *Allāh* per verse, which separates them from long-verse AR surahs such as Q 6, 13, 35 and 46 (pp. 78–79). Exclusive vocabulary includes *tawrāt*, *injīl*, *masīḥ*, *ummī*, *naṣārā*, *sakīna*, *fatḥ* as "victory", and all references to *ḥajj* and *ʿumra* (p. 80).

He treats Q 2, 3 and 7 as composite, and leaves Q 1 and 112 unplaced, considering them probably later than their MVL suggests. He then explains the shifts epigraphically: *Raḥmānān* is the name of God in Jewish and Christian South Arabian inscriptions, while *Allāh* and its variants have a longer northern history, so the Qur'an's naming moves from a southern to a northern preference, the latter "better known to the people of the central Ḥijāz" (p. 10 and ch. 3). This repository encodes his phases (as we read them) in `data/dost_phases.csv` and tests them in notebook 5.

### The three variables in Dost's project: what the literature already says

*Mean verse length* is the best-studied marker; see sections 2–4.

*Divine names.* This is the variable Dost has already developed furthest (*Before the Qur'an*, ch. 3, above). Nöldeke already used one as a dating criterion. He says that in the second Meccan period Muhammad "started to introduce" al-Raḥmān as a proper name for God alongside Allāh; it appears some 50 times, most often in Q 19 (sixteen times), mostly vanishes in the third Meccan period, and is absent from the Medinan surahs (Nöldeke et al., *History of the Qurʾān*, trans. Behn, vol. 1, p. 99 = German i/121; read in the Behn translation). Sinai plots the occurrence of al-Raḥmān against MVL in his Handbook chapter (Sinai 2017, ch. 5 n. 20, citing "Inner-Qur'anic Chronology," Figure 6). One dissenting Turkish-language article argues that Western use of al-Raḥmān as a middle-Meccan marker is prejudiced (Türcan 2016, *Hitit Üniversitesi İlahiyat Fakültesi Dergisi*) **[UNVERIFIED: only the abstract was seen; full citation not checked]**. Before Dost, Alford Welch (EI², "Allāh and Other Supernatural Beings") and Neal Robinson (*Discovering the Qur'an*, p. 91) observed that *Allāh* is absent from the earliest surahs (cited in Dost, p. 72). Dost's chapter 3 is the first systematic treatment of the rabb → Allāh shift as a periodizing criterion.

*Foreign vocabulary.* The standard inventory is Arthur Jeffery, *The Foreign Vocabulary of the Qur'ān* (Baroda: Oriental Institute, 1938; Gaekwad's Oriental Series 79; xv + 311 pp.; reprinted Leiden: Brill, 2007, Texts and Studies on the Qurʾān 3, [10.1163/ej.9789004153523.i-313](https://doi.org/10.1163/ej.9789004153523.i-313)). Jeffery is an etymological dictionary, not a chronology. I found no published study that uses the distribution of loanwords as a dating variable. A practical caution for the project: Jeffery's "foreign origin" judgements are exactly what Dost's own epigraphic work contests. Some words Jeffery derives from Syriac, Dost reads as Peninsular. So the variable's values depend on which etymologies you accept.

**What the method is (from Sinai 2017, ch. 5):**

1. *Measurement.* Sinai counts the Latin letters needed to transliterate each verse, using Hans Zirker's transliteration with the Kufan verse division and the Ḥafṣ ʿan ʿĀṣim reading. Verse endings are put into pausal form, and hyphens and spaces are not counted (ch. 1, n. 47). For each surah he reports the MVL, the standard deviation, and the *coefficient of variation* (standard deviation ÷ mean). The coefficient of variation shows how uneven verse lengths are inside a surah, independent of how long they are on average. For the whole Qurʾān it is 0.76, and only five surahs exceed it, so verse length is much more uniform inside surahs than across the corpus (Figure 10, pp. 114–117).
2. *Removing insertions.* Some short-verse surahs contain a verse or two that are much longer and that he (following Neuwirth) treats as later additions, e.g. Q 73:20 and Q 74:31. He lists such surahs twice, with and without the suspected insertion ("73*", n. 14). Q 73 has an MVL of 63.9 with v. 20 and 41.1 without it. **This matters for code:** a classifier trained on Tanzil's whole-surah labels never sees this layering.
3. *Smooth continuum.* Sorted by MVL, surahs run from about 18 letters (Q 114, Q 112) to about 179 (Q 60), "connected by a fairly continuous upward slope" rather than discrete bands (p. 118). Sinai, following Sadeghi, reads the absence of bands as consistent with gradual development.
4. *Correlation with independent features.* The argument does not rest on verse length alone. Other features line up with MVL: introductory oaths and temporal clauses ("When the sun is wrapped up…") occur only in short-verse surahs; the mysterious isolated letters, superscripts about "the Scripture" and its "sending down," doxologies, and opening vocatives occur only further up the scale. References to the *munāfiqūn* ("hypocrites") appear only in surahs with MVL above about 92. Commands to "fight," "obey God and His Messenger," and sustained polemic against Jews and Christians occur only in long-verse surahs, and formulaic density rises with MVL (pp. 118–122, Figure 13). Sadeghi's morpheme study shows the same covariance at the level of passages rather than whole surahs (p. 122). The inference: so many independent features shifting together "demands an explanation," and the simplest one is gradual literary development over time.
5. *Direction.* That verses got longer, rather than shorter, is established separately: later insertions into short-verse surahs are long-versed, and some long-verse passages refer back to short-verse ones, e.g. Q 9:114 commenting on the Abraham passages in Q 14, 19 and 26 (p. 123).
6. *Limits.* Sinai says plainly that ordering by MVL "cannot simply be equated with a relative chronology." Q 75 (26.80) versus Q 56 (26.78) is noise. Q 70 refers back to Q 52 even though its MVL is about 6.5 letters lower. He wants statistical confidence intervals and non-quantitative arguments as well (pp. 123–124).

**Status of the Meccan/Medinan divide.** Sinai defends it without relying on the post-Qurʾānic surah lists. Surahs that presuppose what he calls the "Medinan constellation" (the community living in *al-madīnah* after expulsion from the sanctuary, in armed conflict with its occupants) also share high MVL, high formulaic density, vocatives like "O you who believe," explicit demarcation from Jews and Christians, the Messenger called *nabiyy* and paired with God in demands for obedience, and legal regulation. Of the fourteen surahs with the highest MVL, thirteen are Medinan for Weil and Nöldeke (pp. 124–126). He adds that this divide would hold "even if one were to doubt" that *al-madīnah* is present-day Medina, though such a sceptic might pick a different label (p. 126). Mixed surahs are explained by redaction: Medinan insertions into Meccan surahs (Q 6, 7, 16, 73, 74), and older Meccan material taken into a Medinan composition (Q 22, where Nöldeke–Schwally's division yields an MVL of 95.07 for the Meccan parts and 119.33 for the Medinan parts; pp. 127–130).

**Hard cases he concedes** (pp. 130–132). Q 98 has Medinan-style polemic but a moderate MVL (74.38), "a genuine puzzle." Q 109 and Q 110 have short verses but use *dīn* in the later sense of "religion"; he suggests these communal prayer texts may stand outside the trend, so that for them MVL reflects genre rather than date. The general caveat is that verse length is a proxy for date only within a single genre.

**Response to sceptics.** In 2017 his position is that assuming verses lengthened over time fits "the basic scaffolding of the traditional narrative," which he counts as a merit until there is "compelling further evidence" to overturn it (p. 123, citing "The Qurʾan as Process," pp. 415–16). He also criticises Weil and Nöldeke's original rationale (the romantic idea of fading prophetic inspiration, inherited from Ewald) while keeping their "Archimedean point," verse length (pp. 112–113). His 2024 *JIQSA* article accepts that Dye, Tesei and Shoemaker have identified a real problem, namely the lack of evidence for organised Christian communities in the pre-Islamic Hijaz. He argues the problem is "less acute" than they say and that their alternative scenario creates explanatory difficulties of its own (abstract via Crossref).

## 3. Behnam Sadeghi, "The Chronology of the Qurʾān: A Stylometric Research Program"

*Arabica* 58, nos. 3–4 (2011): 210–299. [10.1163/157005810X529692](https://doi.org/10.1163/157005810X529692). I read the abstract and two detailed secondary accounts (Sinai 2017; Klar 2017); I did not have the full article.

**Method.** Sadeghi starts from Bazargan's division of the text (section 4) into 194 blocks ordered by verse length. Because the corpus is small (about 78,000 word tokens, versus 4.7 million for Dickens), sampling error is large, so he merges the blocks into 22 groups and then into **seven phases**. Five are roughly Meccan: Group 2 (blocks 17–34), Group 3 (blocks 1, 35–65), Group 4 (66–82), Group 5 (83–91), Groups 6–11 (93–131). Two are Medinan: Groups 12–19 (132–174) and Groups 20–22 (175–194) (Klar 2017, p. 65). He then tests what he calls **concurrent smoothness**: if a proposed order is chronological, several style markers chosen independently of one another should all change smoothly across it. The markers are (1) average verse length; (2) the relative frequencies of the 28 most common morphemes (e.g. *wa*, *inna*, *alladhīna*); (3) 114 other common morphemes (e.g. *rabb*, *man*, *thumma*); and (4) 3,693 relatively uncommon morphemes, those occurring more than once but fewer than twenty times (abstract; Klar 2017, p. 64). He uses the Quranic Arabic Corpus morphological segmentation.

**Findings.** All four markers vary smoothly over the seven phases. This verifies a seven-phase relative chronology, shows the stylistic unity of many long passages, and, Sadeghi claims, shows the Qurʾān has one author (abstract). The Medinan phases show a less clear stylistic trajectory than the Meccan ones, though he places both after the Meccan material (Klar 2017, p. 64). He also argues that interpolations into short-verse surahs being long-versed shows the direction of change was from short to long (Sinai 2017, p. 123, citing Sadeghi p. 283).

**Relation to Nöldeke and Bazargan.** Sadeghi's analysis is built on Bazargan's blocks, recast into phases. Both he and Bazargan warn against reading the block order as precise. Sadeghi tells readers to "resist the temptation" of taking either list more precisely than intended, because statistics support conclusions about averages over groups rather than individual items (quoted in Klar 2017, p. 65). His overall phase structure agrees with the Weil–Nöldeke picture of short-verse early material and long-verse Medinan material. Because the analysis works on blocks, it does not assume surahs are unitary, which is why Sinai treats it as stronger evidence than whole-surah MVL (Sinai 2017, p. 122).

**Criticism.** Smooth covariance establishes an ordering, and the direction of that ordering and its identification with *time* still need further argument. The test also depends on blocks that were defined partly by verse length, which is itself one of the markers being tested. A 2017 Persian-language article critiques the statistical procedure of Bazargan's chronology (Pahlavan, Eqbal and Heidari, *Quranic Doctrines* 13, no. 24, 2017: 185–206) **[UNVERIFIED: cited only via Wikipedia; not seen]**.

## 4. Bazargan, Nöldeke–Schwally, Bell, Blachère, Neuwirth, and the Cairo edition

**Mehdi Bazargan.** *Sayr-i taḥawwul-i Qurʾān* ("The Evolution of the Qurʾān"), Persian, with French parallel title *Évolution du Coran*. Klar describes it as "a series of works published in the late 1970s and early 1980s." Bazargan left 59 surahs intact and split the other 55 into two to five blocks each, 194 blocks in total. His main criterion was verse length, with breaks placed partly by thematic unity, rhyme, and historical information. Blocks were ordered by a calculation combining mean verse length, modal verse length, and the share of verses at the mode (Klar 2017, pp. 64–65, citing vol. 2, pp. 1–7). Bibliographic details are only partly verified: Wikipedia gives 1976; a Penn Libraries record shows a 3rd edition, Tehran: Shirkat-i Sahāmī-i Intishār / Qalam, 1358 Sh./1979, ed. Muḥammad Mahdī Jaʿfarī **[UNVERIFIED: first-edition date and publisher, number of volumes]**.

**Nöldeke–Schwally.** Theodor Nöldeke, *Geschichte des Qorâns* (Göttingen, 1860), which grew out of a Latin prize essay of 1856. The second edition was revised by Friedrich Schwally (vol. 1 1909, vol. 2 1919), with vol. 3 by Gotthelf Bergsträßer and Otto Pretzl (1926–1938) (Reynolds 2011, p. 478). English: Theodor Nöldeke, Friedrich Schwally, Gotthelf Bergsträßer and Otto Pretzl, *The History of the Qurʾān*, ed. and trans. Wolfgang H. Behn (Leiden: Brill, 2013), [10.1163/9789004228795](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004228795). The scheme, which Nöldeke took over from Gustav Weil (1844), has four groups: **Meccan I (48 surahs), Meccan II (21), Meccan III (21), Medinan (24)**. The group lists are from the Wikipedia article on the *Geschichte*; I spot-checked placements against the Behn text (Q 99 discussed under Meccan I, Q 76 under Meccan II, Q 13 under Meccan III). Nöldeke himself judged the traditional revelation-order list, "possibly stretching back to Ibn ʿAbbās," to be "a rough attempt… on the basis of an extremely uncritical foundation." He noted that the traditional lists disagree about many more surahs than the tradition admits, and said the reliable guide is the Qurʾān's own diction, especially verse length (*History*, vol. 1, pp. 50–51; read).

**Richard Bell.** *The Qur'ān, Translated, with a Critical Re-arrangement of the Surahs*, 2 vols. (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1937–1939), following Flügel's text and verse numbering. Bell kept the surah order but marked, by typography, his dissection of almost every surah into separately dated fragments, which assumes heavy editorial assembly. Bell's views were systematised in W. Montgomery Watt, *Bell's Introduction to the Qur'ān* (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1970; reissued 2021, [10.1515/9781474497008](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781474497008)). Sinai rejects Bell's near-total fragmentation but grants that some surahs combine pre-existing units (2017, pp. 113, 128).

**Régis Blachère.** *Le Coran: traduction selon un essai de reclassement des sourates*, Islam d'hier et d'aujourd'hui (Paris: G.-P. Maisonneuve). Introduction volume 1947 (273 pp.), vol. I 1949 (pp. 1–536), vol. II 1951 (pp. 537–1240), per a contemporary review listing. The translation is arranged in a chronological order based largely on the German school (Nöldeke–Schwally). Later reprints are in Qurʾānic order.

**Angelika Neuwirth.** *Studien zur Komposition der mekkanischen Suren* (Berlin: de Gruyter, 1981; 2nd ed. 2007, [10.1515/9783110920383](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110920383)). She keeps Nöldeke's three Meccan periods but bases her chronology on how surahs are built: rhyme, verse groups, and compositional structure. Her pp. 11–63 on verse-division variants is the model Sinai cites for redoing MVL with critically evaluated verse breaks (Sinai 2017, ch. 5 n. 13). *Der Koran als Text der Spätantike* (Berlin: Verlag der Weltreligionen, 2010) was translated as *The Qur'an and Late Antiquity: A Shared Heritage*, trans. Samuel Wilder (New York: OUP, 2019), [10.1093/oso/9780199928958.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780199928958.001.0001). It reads the Qurʾān as a liturgical text that developed over time inside a community, and its diachronic reading (e.g., Medinan rereadings of Meccan Q 19) is one target of Reynolds's 2011 critique. Her chronological commentary is *The Qur'an: Text and Commentary, Volume 1: Early Meccan Suras* (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2022), [10.12987/9780300265545](https://doi.org/10.12987/9780300265545), with a vol. 2.1 in 2024. It includes an appendix listing "Secondary Additions to the Early Meccan Suras."

**The Cairo/Egyptian 1924 edition and where its chronology comes from.** The 1924 Cairo ("King Fuad" / Amīriyya) printing gives, in each surah header, Meccan or Medinan, its position in an order of revelation, and in many cases a list of exceptional verses (e.g., Q 73 "Meccan except vv. 10, 11 and 20, from Medina"). The later Medina muṣḥaf dropped the order-of-revelation information, according to Corpus Coranicum's summary (relayed by Wikipedia). The order is generally described as the list attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās (Böwering, "Chronology and the Qurʾān," *Encyclopaedia of the Qurʾān*, vol. 1 [Brill, 2001], relayed via Wikipedia). Neal Robinson reports that it matches a version given in a 15th-century work by ʿAbd al-Kāfī (Robinson, *Discovering the Qur'an*, 2nd ed., 2003, pp. 25–97, relayed via Wikipedia) **[UNVERIFIED at source: I did not read Böwering's EQ article or Robinson directly, and I could not establish which pre-modern text the 1924 committee actually copied the verse-level exceptions from]**. More generally, the traditional material behind such headers consists of revelation-order lists and *asbāb al-nuzūl* ("occasions of revelation") reports. Sinai cites al-Suyūṭī's *al-Itqān*, nawʿ 1, for the classical Meccan/Medinan discussion, and Andrew Rippin's work on how *asbāb* reports function as exegesis rather than independent historical record (Sinai 2017, ch. 5 nn. 1–3). The scholarly consequence: these labels are early *interpretation*, not independent evidence, and Nöldeke already said so.

## 5. Critiques of the chronological paradigm

**Gabriel Said Reynolds**, "Le problème de la chronologie du Coran," *Arabica* 58, no. 6 (2011): 477–502, [10.1163/157005811X587903](https://doi.org/10.1163/157005811X587903) (read). He traces the "practically axiomatic" chronological reading from Islamic tradition through Weil and Nöldeke, and argues that it assumes one author, no redactor, and a text mirroring Muhammad's community at Mecca and Medina in 610–632. He criticises its recent defenders and compares the Meccan/Medinan surah headings to the Psalm superscriptions, which biblical scholars now treat as later exegesis. His conclusion: chronology is "plausible" but "far from established," reading by chronology means reading by the *Sīra* (the Prophet's biography), and since the Sīra is itself exegetical the reasoning risks a "vicious circle." He asks whether the Qurʾān itself requires a Meccan/Medinan reading. The circularity point bears directly on any classifier trained on the traditional labels.

**Emmanuelle Stefanidis**, "The Qur'an Made Linear: A Study of the *Geschichte des Qorâns*' Chronological Reordering," *Journal of Qur'anic Studies* 10, no. 2 (2008): 1–22, [10.3366/E1465359109000394](https://doi.org/10.3366/E1465359109000394). It examines what Nöldeke's reordering does to the text as a reading experience. Her later "In Search of Chronology: Narratives of Qur'anic Evolution in Western Academia," *ReOrient* 9, no. 1 (2024), [10.13169/reorient.9.1.0070](https://doi.org/10.13169/reorient.9.1.0070) (pp. 70–95 per Wikipedia; Crossref gives no pages), says the question "appears to have reached an impasse" and argues for treating diachronic readings, with Neuwirth and Sinai as case studies, as interpretive constructions rather than positivist reconstructions.

**Stephen J. Shoemaker**, *Creating the Qur'an: A Historical-Critical Study* (Oakland: University of California Press, 2022), open access, [10.1525/luminos.128](https://doi.org/10.1525/luminos.128). He argues the text was fixed only under ʿAbd al-Malik and al-Ḥajjāj, after decades of oral transmission subject to the memory distortions he documents from memory science. He also argues that the Qurʾān's engagement with Christian and Jewish traditions fits poorly with what is known of the late antique Hijaz, and that much of the corpus therefore took shape after the conquests and outside Mecca/Medina. On this view an inner chronology mapped onto Muhammad's career loses its anchor. (Chapter titles verified via Crossref; summary from reviews and Sinai's abstract.)

**Guillaume Dye.** He treats the Qurʾān as a *corpus* of texts with distinct compositional layers, to be analysed by form and redaction criticism independently of the "Nöldeke paradigm" (summary of his position in *Le Coran des historiens*, ed. Mohammad Ali Amir-Moezzi and Guillaume Dye, 3 vols., Paris: Cerf, 2019). He also argues for multiple contexts, some post-prophetic ("Quelques questions sur les contextes du Coran," *Archimède* 10, 2023). On chronology specifically: "The Qur'anic Mary and the Chronology of the Qur'ān," in G. Dye (ed.), *Early Islam: The Sectarian Milieu of Late Antiquity?* (Brussels: Éditions de l'Université de Bruxelles, Problèmes d'histoire des religions 29, 2022/2023; open access via OAPEN) **[UNVERIFIED: chapter page range; publication year differs between catalogues]**. He argues Q 19's Mary pericope reflects a post-conquest Palestinian milieu, a claim Sinai's 2024 appendix rebuts.

**Tommaso Tesei**, "'The Romans Will Win!' Q 30:2–7 in Light of 7th c. Political Eschatology," *Der Islam* 95, no. 1 (2018): 1–29, [10.1515/islam-2018-0001](https://doi.org/10.1515/islam-2018-0001). He reads the passage, traditionally Meccan, as part of early 7th-century apocalyptic propaganda around the Byzantine–Sasanian war and as a *vaticinium ex eventu* (a "prophecy" written after the event), which would put it after Heraclius's victory of 628. This is the type of argument that dates single passages by external events rather than by style.

**Joseph Witztum**, "Variant Traditions, Relative Chronology, and the Study of Intra-Quranic Parallels," in Behnam Sadeghi, Asad Q. Ahmed, Adam Silverstein and Robert Hoyland (eds.), *Islamic Cultures, Islamic Contexts: Essays in Honor of Professor Patricia Crone* (Leiden: Brill, 2015), 1–50, [10.1163/9789004281714_002](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004281714_002). Witztum is not a sceptic of chronology as such. He asks when differences between parallel versions of the same story show that one version depends on another, and when they reflect independent variant traditions. This sets limits on the "later passage reworks earlier passage" argument that Sinai and Neuwirth rely on. **[Content summary is from secondary descriptions; I did not read the chapter.]**

**Verse counts and codices: why the MVL numbers are not fixed.** Early Muslim scholarship preserved several regional verse-count systems (Kufan, Basran, Damascene, Ḥimṣī, Meccan, Medinan I and II). Hythem Sidky, "On the Regionality of Qurʾānic Codices," *JIQSA* 5 (2020): 133–210, [10.5913/jiqsa.5.2020.a005](https://doi.org/10.5913/jiqsa.5.2020.a005), uses these systems with other features to locate early manuscripts regionally. Raymond K. Farrin, "A Revised Inner-Qurʾanic Chronology Based on Mean Verse Lengths and the Medina I Counting System," *Al-Abhath* 67, no. 1 (2019): 1–29, [10.1163/18115586-67010001](https://doi.org/10.1163/18115586-67010001), recomputes MVL with the Medina I count, which he argues is closer to an original system than the Kufan count printed today. He gets three periods (Early Meccan, Later Meccan, Medinan) and argues for an early Meccan nucleus (abstract). Related: Marijn van Putten, "'The Grace of God' as Evidence for a Written Uthmanic Archetype," *BSOAS* 82, no. 2 (2019): 271–288, [10.1017/S0041977X19000338](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X19000338). It shows that the standard consonantal text goes back to a single written archetype, which supports treating today's text as a stable object to measure. See also his *Quranic Arabic* (Brill, 2022), [10.1163/9789004506251](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004506251), and Sinai, "Beyond the Cairo Edition," *JAOS* 140, no. 1 (2020) **[UNVERIFIED: page range; Crossref omits it]**.

## 6. Computational and machine-learning studies

**What's here:** a small literature, mostly in computer-science venues outside Qurʾānic studies, mostly evaluated on the traditional labels, and no study I could find that applies transformers to dating and reports an evaluation held out by surah.

- **Abubakar Abid (2017)**, "Can a Machine Learn to Classify Meccan and Medinan Surahs?", blog post, 16 Jan 2017 ([abidlabs.github.io](https://abidlabs.github.io/Classifying-Surahs-Meccan-Medinan/)), code at [github.com/abidlabs/classify-surahs](https://github.com/abidlabs/classify-surahs). This repository began as Abid's code; his files are preserved in `legacy/`. Unvowelled Tanzil text; labels from Tanzil's revelation-order page; bag of words over each verse (14,870 types); logistic regression. **The split is by chapter, not by verse** (40% train / 60% validation by surah), chosen so that refrains like Q 55's do not leak across the split. Reported: training 98.68%; verse-level validation 86.26%; surah-level majority vote 94.73%; baseline of labelling every verse Meccan about 74%. The six surahs counted as misclassified, all predicted Meccan, are Q 13, 22, 47, 55, 76, 99. Abid himself notes the verse-inherits-surah-label problem and class imbalance.
- **Mohamadou Nassourou**, "Using Machine Learning Algorithms for Categorizing Quranic Chapters by Major Phases of Prophet Mohammad's Messengership" (University of Würzburg OPUS repository; PDF metadata dated Dec. 2011; not peer reviewed; read). SVM and naive Bayes on stemmed chapter vectors. Trained on chapters with "unanimously agreed" places of revelation, then grown iteratively by adding classified chapters to the training set (self-training). He reports "precision" of 90.29% (SVM) and 68.93% (NB), but computes it as overall correct/total, so the number is really accuracy and is mislabelled. SVM initially missed Q 99, 13, 55, 76, 98, 59, 63, 49, 66, 62 as Medinan. NB persistently assigned Q 99, 76 and 55 to Meccan, and he concluded they "in fact" look Meccan. A companion paper on reconstructing chronology with a hybrid classifier is also in OPUS (urn:nbn:de:bvb:20-opus-54712) **[not read]**.
- **Mohammed Akour, Izzat Alsmadi and Iyad Alazzam**, "MQVC: Measuring Quranic Verses Similarity and Sura Classification Using N-Gram," *WSEAS Transactions on Computers* 13 (2014): 485–491. N-grams plus LibSVM (Weka) to classify chapters as Makki/Madani **[UNVERIFIED: accuracy figures; full text not read]**.
- **Bassam Arkok and Akram M. Zeki**, "Classification of Holy Quran Verses based on Imbalanced Learning," *International Journal on Islamic Applications in Computer Science and Technology* 8, no. 2 (June 2020): 11–24 (read). Meccan/Medinan chapters, 10-fold cross-validation, SMOTE oversampling. Without SMOTE, accuracy was about 0.82–0.85, but specificity on the minority class was only 0.39–0.57, which shows high accuracy hiding failure on the smaller class. Average specificity rose from 0.42 to 0.88 with SMOTE. The paper calls SMOTE "a pre-processing step before the classification phase" and does not say it was applied inside each fold. If it was applied to the whole dataset before splitting, synthetic training points were built from test chapters, a standard form of leakage, so the post-SMOTE figures should not be trusted until that is ruled out. It also mixes up which class is the minority (it says it oversamples "Meccan chapters," which are the majority).
- **Hermann Moisl**, "Sura Length and Lexical Probability Estimation in Cluster Analysis of the Qur'an," *ACM Transactions on Asian Language Information Processing* 8, no. 4 (2009): article 19 (pp. 1–19), [10.1145/1644879.1644886](https://doi.org/10.1145/1644879.1644886). Unsupervised clustering of surahs by word frequency, extending Thabet (2005). Shows that short surahs cannot be reliably placed because their word-frequency estimates are statistically too noisy, and derives a minimum-length threshold from sampling theory. This sets a hard limit for any bag-of-words method applied to surahs like Q 99, 108 or 112.
- **Not verified, so not used for claims:** a 2025-ish Quranica (University of Malaya) "Multi-Dimensional NLP Analysis of the Quran" using TF-IDF and AraBERT to profile Meccan/Medinan surahs; an Iranian tf-idf/word2vec study of surah ordering (ensani.ir); an Indonesian MA thesis applying Sadeghi's concurrent-smoothness test to Q 13, 22, 76 (UIN Sunan Kalijaga). I found no ACL Anthology or arXiv paper on Meccan/Medinan classification or surah dating with transformers.

**Methodological weaknesses across this literature:**

1. *Circular labels.* Every supervised study trains on the Cairo/Tanzil labels, which are themselves early exegesis (Reynolds; Nöldeke). A classifier that "agrees with tradition 95% of the time" has learned to reproduce a medieval classification. It has not found an independent date. Disagreements are not automatically model errors; for Q 13, 55, 76, 99, Nöldeke agrees with the model rather than with Tanzil (see below).
2. *Leakage.* Random verse-level splits put neighbouring verses, shared refrains, and formulae from one surah on both sides of the split. Abid avoided this; the risk returns as soon as students use `train_test_split` on verses. The same applies to oversampling before cross-validation.
3. *Label granularity.* Whole-surah labels ignore the verse-level exceptions the Cairo headers themselves list, and ignore the insertions Sinai and Neuwirth identify.
4. *Length confound.* Medinan verses are much longer, so verse-level bag-of-words models get far more evidence per Medinan verse. Verse length is also itself the main chronological marker, so a classifier can be "right" about chronology through length alone. A length-only baseline (logistic regression on MVL) should be reported next to any lexical model.
5. *Imbalance and metrics.* With 86 Meccan and 28 Medinan surahs (Tanzil), always guessing "Meccan" scores about 75% at surah level. Report balanced accuracy or per-class recall.
6. *Pretraining contamination (inference, not tested).* CAMeLBERT-CA was pretrained on OpenITI classical Arabic texts. That corpus is very likely to include the Qurʾān itself and *tafsīr* and *asbāb al-nuzūl* works that discuss which verses are Meccan or Medinan, so a fine-tuned model may partly retrieve exegetical associations rather than learn style.

## 7. Datasets and tools

**Tanzil** ([tanzil.net](https://tanzil.net)). The text license page says Creative Commons Attribution 3.0, but adds that verbatim copying is allowed and "changing it is not allowed," requires the source to be indicated, and asks for a link to tanzil.net ([text license](https://tanzil.net/docs/text_license)). The no-modification clause conflicts with CC BY, which normally permits adaptation. Cite it, keep the header, and do not ship an altered text file. Normalised or stripped versions generated at runtime by student code are the safer pattern. Metadata: `quran-data.xml` (attribute `license="cc-by"`) gives per surah `ayas`, `start`, Arabic and English names, **`type` (Meccan/Medinan)** and **`order` (revelation order)**, plus juz/hizb/manzil/ruku/page/sajda tables ([metadata page](https://tanzil.net/docs/quran_metadata); file downloaded and inspected). It lists 28 Medinan surahs, including 13, 22, 47, 55, 76, 98, 99 and 110 (order 114). Its [revelation-order page](https://tanzil.net/docs/revelation_order) says the order comes from narrations going back to Ibn ʿAbbās, with details taken from al-Zanjānī's *History of the Qurʾān*. Its "Note" column encodes **verse-level exceptions**, e.g. Q 68 "Except 17–33 and 48–50, from Medina"; Q 73 "Except 10, 11 and 20"; Q 2 "Except 281 from Mina at the time of the Last Hajj"; Q 8 "Except 30–36 from Mecca." This is the most accessible machine-readable source of verse exceptions I found, though it is only on the HTML page and not in the XML. **Error in this source:** the page says Nöldeke's order differs from the traditional one in only two places (Q 110 and Q 62). That is false: in Nöldeke, Q 13, 55, 76 and 99 (all Medinan in Tanzil) are Meccan. Do not use Tanzil's page as a source for Nöldeke.

**Quranic Arabic Corpus** (Kais Dukes), [corpus.quran.com](https://corpus.quran.com). The download page offers morphology version 0.4 (2011) after entering an email address. The file header says GNU General Public License, but also requires verbatim copying ("changing it is not allowed"), attribution, and a link to corpus.quran.com. The same licensing tension as Tanzil applies. Papers: Kais Dukes and Nizar Habash, "Morphological Annotation of Quranic Arabic," *LREC 2010*; Dukes, Atwell and Habash, "Supervised Collaboration for Syntactic Annotation of Quranic Arabic," *Language Resources and Evaluation* 47, no. 1 (2013; online 2011): 33–62, [10.1007/s10579-011-9167-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10579-011-9167-7). Mirror: [github.com/q-ran/quran](https://github.com/q-ran/quran) repackages QAC 0.4 plus Tanzil as a Text-Fabric dataset (data CC BY 4.0, per its README). I found no verified GitHub mirror of the raw `quranic-corpus-morphology-0.4.txt`, and given the verbatim-only terms, downloading from the official page is the clean route. Sadeghi used QAC segmentation for his morpheme counts.

**Nöldeke ordering in machine-readable form.** I found no maintained CSV/JSON of Nöldeke's (or Bell's or Blachère's) order on GitHub. This repository builds its 114-row table from Robinson's tabulation as reproduced on Wikipedia (see `data/SOURCES.md`); its period sizes match Nöldeke–Schwally's (Meccan I 48, II 21, III 21, Medinan 24), and spot-checks against Behn's translation are advisable. For MVL, Sinai 2017 Figure 10 (pp. 114–117) gives per-surah MVL, SD, CV, and the "asterisk" versions without insertions, which is enough to check student computations. Verse-level exceptions: Tanzil's revelation-order notes (above).

**Arabic NLP models and tools (ACL Anthology–verified):**

- **CAMeL Tools**: Ossama Obeid et al., "CAMeL Tools: An Open Source Python Toolkit for Arabic Natural Language Processing," *LREC 2020*, 7022–7032, [aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.868](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.868/). Morphological analysis, dediacritisation, tokenisation.
- **CAMeLBERT-CA**: [`CAMeL-Lab/bert-base-arabic-camelbert-ca`](https://huggingface.co/CAMeL-Lab/bert-base-arabic-camelbert-ca), Apache-2.0. Pretrained on about 6 GB (847M words) of classical Arabic from OpenITI v2020.1.2, with diacritics removed. Paper: Go Inoue, Bashar Alhafni, Nurpeiis Baimukan, Houda Bouamor and Nizar Habash, "The Interplay of Variant, Size, and Task Type in Arabic Pre-trained Language Models," *WANLP 2021*, 92–104, [aclanthology.org/2021.wanlp-1.10](https://aclanthology.org/2021.wanlp-1.10/). See contamination caveat in section 6.
- **AraBERT**: Wissam Antoun, Fady Baly and Hazem Hajj, "AraBERT: Transformer-based Model for Arabic Language Understanding," *OSACT 4 (LREC 2020 workshop)*, 9–15, [aclanthology.org/2020.osact-1.2](https://aclanthology.org/2020.osact-1.2/). Trained on Modern Standard Arabic news/web text, so it is a poorer fit for Qurʾānic Arabic than CAMeLBERT-CA.
- **Quran-specific embeddings:** I found none that I could verify as a maintained, documented model. Qurʾānic QA shared-task systems (e.g., Qur'an QA 2023) fine-tune general Arabic BERTs **[not investigated further]**.

**For a course where students mostly read English translations:** English translations are poor inputs for these methods. Verse length in English depends on the translator, and translators impose their own interpretation (including, in some, chronological notes). Arabic text plus transliteration-based MVL, as in Sinai, is the defensible measurement. English is fine for close-reading the passages a model flags.

### What this means for this repository

Abid's six "misclassified" surahs line up with known scholarly disagreements, not with model noise. Q 13, 55, 76 and 99 are Medinan in Tanzil/Cairo but **Meccan in Nöldeke** (Q 55 and 99 Meccan I, Q 76 Meccan II, Q 13 Meccan III). Q 22 is Medinan in both, but Sinai shows it is a composite with a large Meccan layer (about 5,100 Meccan to 2,800 Medinan transliteration letters). Q 47 is Medinan in both. Swapping in Nöldeke's labels, or excluding disputed surahs, and comparing results is a direct way to show students how much the "accuracy" depends on which chronology you call ground truth.

## Consensus vs live disagreement

**Points of broad scholarly agreement** (among historical-critical scholars, including most sceptics):

- The canonical surah order is not the order of proclamation; the tradition itself says so.
- Verse length varies enormously across the corpus (MVL from about 18 to about 179 transliteration letters) and much less within surahs.
- Verse length co-varies with many independent features: opening devices, vocabulary profile, formulaic density, legal content, addressees (Sinai 2017; Sadeghi 2011). Nobody disputes the correlation; the dispute is over what produced it.
- A distinct "Medinan" sub-corpus with shared stylistic, doctrinal and situational traits can be identified from the text itself, whatever one calls it.
- Many surahs are composite: later insertions into short surahs (e.g., Q 73:20, Q 74:31) and earlier material absorbed into later ones (e.g., Q 22).
- The traditional Meccan/Medinan labels and revelation-order lists are exegetical products, inconsistent with one another in detail, and not independent historical evidence (Nöldeke already; Reynolds; Sinai).
- Fine-grained orderings within a period, or of surahs with similar MVL, are not recoverable from verse length alone.

**Points of live disagreement:**

- Whether the covariance reflects *time* within one prophet's career (Sinai, Sadeghi, Neuwirth), or could reflect genre, setting, or multiple contexts and redactors (Reynolds, Dye, Shoemaker).
- Whether the corpus was substantially fixed by about 650 (the majority, supported by van Putten's archetype evidence and radiocarbon-dated early manuscripts) or shaped into the 690s and later (Shoemaker, Dye).
- Whether "Meccan" and "Medinan" should be tied to those places at all (Sinai says the textual divide survives even without the geography; Dye and Tesei locate some material after the conquests).
- How many phases there are and where the boundaries fall: Nöldeke's 3+1, Sadeghi's 5+2, Farrin's 2+1, Dost's three divine-name clusters, Bazargan's 194 blocks.
- Which verse-count system to measure with (Kufan vs Medina I) and which verses to excise as insertions. Both change MVL values.
- The direction and reliability of intra-Qurʾānic dependence arguments (Sinai/Neuwirth vs Witztum's caution about variant traditions).
- Whether a single author can be inferred from stylometric homogeneity (Sadeghi claims so; contested).
- Placement of specific surahs: Q 13, 55, 76, 98, 99, 109, 110 are the recurring hard cases.
